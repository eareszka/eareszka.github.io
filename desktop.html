About

A top-down RPG I'm writing in C++17 with SDL2, with no engine. Every seed generates a 3000 by 3000 tile world: biomes, rivers, cliffs, fifteen settlements joined by roads, and nine kinds of dungeon. Fights are bullet-hell battles. I started it in April 2026 to practice procedural generation.

What went wrong along the way, and how I found it, is in the dev log.

Newest first. Every number here was measured, mostly by small tools I wrote to render or count the world without opening the game. Pictures come from those same tools.

Nov 8 to 9: The hand, and the morning after

This entry finishes the intro. The dog runs to the north shore and barks at nothing. When you get within seven tiles, a page drifts down out of the air, swaying, and lands in front of its nose. The dog circles it, barking at it from every side, and only then can you pick it up. The note reads: "Tonight the last pet is taken. Tomorrow something new takes its place."

A moment after you read it, a hand comes up out of the sea. It's enemy 44, Itqiirpak, drawn at the same size as its real fight but filled solid black, and cut off at the waterline as it rises over three seconds. It breathes, then opens every mouth on its palm at once. The dog turns and barks at it, and the boy can't move. The water fits: in its lore, 44 comes out of the water.

A fight you're meant to lose

The battle has an intro mode now: 3 bars of health, no bombs, no contact damage, and the enemy's name shows as "???". Itqiirpak opens with a grasp you can't dodge, an outline of a hand that closes on you. It repeats the grasp until you use a bomb, and you don't have one, so the third hit ends it. Bombs were reworked for this: X now spends one and clears every enemy bullet on screen, and nothing else. That's the way out of this exact attack later on. I also found that two overlapping bullets could take two bars in a single frame. Now you can only be hit once per frame.

However the fight ends, the night is over. Back on the map, the boy lies out cold where he stood. The hand reaches down over the shore, closes on the dog and carries it off north, its barking getting fainter. Then the screen goes black.

He wakes up in the everyday version of his room. The plant and the chest are back where the dog's bed and bowl were, as if the dog had never lived there. The curtains stay shut until he goes outside. On his first step out he stands still while the night lifts to day and the fog thins away a step at a time, and then the game is yours.

Making it the real start

NEW GAME now opens on the intro: asleep at home, on foot, with every story flag cleared, so the opening chest is closed again even over an old save. The intro resets fully every time it starts, so a second new game in the same session plays the night from the beginning. You start with 3 bars of health instead of 12, and level-ups still add more. SAVE answers "NOT NOW" during the night, so the intro always plays from the start. Once it's over, "Where's my dog?" is a talk option with everyone who isn't busy. Talk menus now take up to four choices, with the person's own topic first. Nobody on the island remembers the dog. They're surprised to hear he ever had one.

Above: the shore, the fight and the morning. These were captured from the running game, using the intro's test starts (INTRO_OUTSIDE=note, hand and taken), which drop you in partway through the night.

Nov 7 to 8: A foggy night, and a reason to leave home

The game has a story now, or at least its spine. The world's script is written somewhere above it, and the pages fall into the world as notes. One night the writers take the boy's dog. He's the only one who remembers it, so he sets out to get it back. Most of the rest of the story is still undecided, and I'm keeping it off this page.

The intro happens on the north half of the starting island. I drew the area by hand as a mask, where each pixel is a quarter of a tile, starting from the fixed corner of the first town. Everything outside it is hidden under a wall of fog, and the boy slides along its edge instead of walking in. That keeps him on the path between his house and the shore.

Night and fog, two passes

Night is a single pass over the finished frame: every pixel is multiplied by one colour and then has another added. Tiles, sprites and townspeople all darken together, and no art needs a night version. The fog is drawn over the map tile by tile. Each tile's density is its distance to the nearest open tile, in four steps, and each step is a 4×4 Bayer dither in 2-pixel dots. That gives it a hard, NES-style edge instead of a soft blur. In the morning the fog drops one step at a time, so it thins out instead of fading.

The dog is a small dark brown French bulldog with four views. It walks with the same bounce as the boy, and when it's idle it either pants or, from behind, wags its tail. It follows the boy along his own footsteps, both indoors and out. It keeps a short trail of where he's been, so it goes around corners the way he did instead of cutting through walls.

The night has its own version of room 0. The dog's bed sits where the plant usually is, and its bowl sits where the opening chest usually is. The boy and the dog both sleep with Z's above them, and you can't move. After two seconds the dog wakes with a start, looks around and runs out the door, the same way a townsperson leaves a room. A second later the boy stirs, hops out of bed, and the lights come up slowly. Outside, the dog is already well up the path, running north. The day clock stands still for the whole intro, so nobody in town wanders over to the house.

The opening chest

The boy now starts with no weapon, and he can't swing or shoot without one. On the morning after, the chest by the plant holds 2 wood, 3 stone and the knife book, with the line "Someone left these out for you." That's enough to make your first weapon. The chest opens once per game. Its flag went into an unused padding byte in the story flags, so old saves keep their layout. The MAKE menu now also hides recipes whose book you don't own yet.

Above: the mock-up made before any code was written, day against night; the dog's sheet by day and by night; and the room on the night, captured from the game before and after the lights come up.

Nov 4 to 6: One rule for every enemy sprite

The battle sprites had drifted. They were drawn over two months, some by hand, some from Python generators and some from other tools, and side by side they looked like they came from different games. So I picked the 26 sprites I liked best, put them on one board, and wrote down what they have in common. The result is a single page that every enemy sprite now has to pass.

It's NES style at every size: flat colours, a black outline, and every pixel placed on purpose. A big enemy is built the same way as a small one, from distinct closed parts at the same 1-pixel scale as the player. A bigger sprite gets more parts, not bigger plain areas. No pattern repeats exactly: scales and plates change size and spacing, and some are left out. Art is never scaled, and the game draws every sprite at 2×.

Size by tier

An enemy's size now comes from its tier, and each tier starts just above the one before it. Starters are 20 pixels on their longer side, low tier is 21 to 31, medium is 33 to 44, and so on up through the elite tier at 81 to 96. Bosses run from 100 pixels up to 280. Within a tier, how big the creature is in its lore decides where it falls in the range. The frame has to fit the drawing closely, because the hit circle comes from the frame's size.

Then I went back through the roster. Seventy-two sprites were redrawn to the rule and are now in the game as still images while their animations get redone. The old drafts and before-and-after copies, hundreds of files, were deleted. Reference images showing other artists' work are now kept out of the repo.

Above: the style board. The armoured figure is 34 Anaye, the pick the other sprites borrow from most. The hand is 44, Itqiirpak, which comes back in the two entries above.

Oct 27: Something to find in every biome

Outside the towns, the land between dungeons was empty: grass, sand or snow, then trees, then more of the same. Now every biome has props. There are 59 kinds, laid out in themed groups that never mix. The desert gets cactus stands, rock outcrops and old trails with wagons and skulls. The forest gets deadfall, abandoned camps and undergrowth. The plains get farms and Mayan ruins, and the ruins ring the step pyramids.

Snow and the wasteland got props later the same day. Snow has ice spires, lost expeditions and mammoth bones, and the wasteland has volcano fields, rubble and remains. The desert's ground was redrawn as plain sand, and there's a new void ground, which the wasteland's rivers and pools are now made of. The tumbleweed is the only prop that isn't placed: it blows across the desert instead.

Everything breaks

Every prop can be broken for item drops, one blow per swing, and broken props stay broken: the cells are recorded the same way chopped trees are. A check tool, propcheck, builds a world and hits one prop of each kind with a club until it breaks. It confirms each prop took the number of blows its table says, that every cell is gone and stays gone, and that it paid out exactly its drops. Group placement is also tested: on seed 387, desert groups are 31 tiles apart on average, forest groups 38, and no farm comes within 70 tiles of a ruin. Changing the world's contents meant raising the world-generation version. Saves record which version they were played in, and the title screen refuses a save from an older world instead of loading it into a different one.

The enemies that chase you on the map got their own small sprites too, every finished one up to 77, Hoga. Before this, a chaser was just its battle sprite shrunk down. Each mini is a hand-drawn grid, with views generated in PixelLab as a starting point. They each move in their own way: some roll, some drift, some streak towards you, fliers hover, and the Asp was reworked to slither.

Above: the fullest group of eight themes on seed 387, rendered by the shot tool at each group's coordinates. Then the first frame of every map mini, at 3×.

Oct 17: A town that keeps its own hours

The first town has people in it now, and they have somewhere to be. The woodcutter walks to the nearest standing tree, chops it from the side, moves on when it falls, and goes home to bed at night. The mother cooks for an hour, then queues at the book shop behind the grandmother and walks home. The fisher, the courier and the gardener each have their own day, and at night most of them go to bed.

One clock for everything

The world runs on a single clock, a 30-minute day that stops in menus, conversations and battles, so nobody walks off while you're reading. Routines share one pathfinder that prefers the trails. Each routine has its own headless check that plays through the day, confirming that nobody walks through a wall, that everyone turns up where they should, and that saving mid-errand and loading puts them back where they were.

Above: the woodcutter, the gardener, a queue at the book shop and two sleepers, then a conversation. People turn to face you when you talk, and some let you choose what to say.

Oct 15 to 17: Menus and the HUD

The TAB menu, the book shop and the end of a battle all share one look now: the game's own cursor, a blinking arrow when a list goes on, and an icon beside every item. A recipe shows what it upgrades and what it still costs as a row of icons joined by plus signs. Things you make once and things you use up sit in separate columns, so a long list stays readable.

Above: health, level and EXP stay at the top while you explore, and a boss adds its own name and health bar. Unlike the rest of this log, the menu and HUD shots are captured from the running game.

Oct 7: Bullet patterns, EXP and the dungeons' outsides

Enemies 04 to 12, from Lili to Ebigane, each got their own bullet pattern. New kinds of bullet came with them: mines that wait before they go off, boomerangs that come back, and shots that speed up or slow down. Volleys are fired in shapes too: blossoms, eyes, leopard spots and pentagrams. Some enemies fire on a breath frame of their idle animation, so you can read the attack coming.

Scoring a fight

A hit now costs a whole bar of health and is clearly shown, and you can switch weapons mid-fight. EXP is a score of the fight: hits landed, bullets grazed and enemies defeated all count, and mining pays some too. Enough of it levels you up.

Every dungeon now looks like where you came in. Ruins, pyramids and barrows have their own walls inside, doors and ladders lead between floors, and houses and the book shop have interiors.

Above: a graveyard's mausoleum, the inside of the starting house, the book shop and four of the other houses, and stonehenge with its stairway down. The gravestones and the fence can both be broken.

Oct 6: Crafting, and a raft off the first island

Weapons are now made, not found. Each one is a shape plus an ore: the shape decides how it swings or fires, the ore decides its damage, colour and what it can harvest. A TAB menu lists what you carry, what you can make and what each recipe still needs. Special parts come from the ruins, stonehenge and the catacombs.

The opening

You start on an island ringed by water. To leave you build a raft from wood, vine taken from a Treesqueak and the raft book, and it sails straight until it hits land. The two dungeons on the starting island only hold the weakest enemies and never link to each other, and the graveyard's way down stays hidden until you break its gravestone. A small checker tool, craftcheck, confirms every recipe can actually be finished from what the world gives you.

Oct 4: Walking behind a church

Every dungeon entrance got its own building: ruins, a mausoleum, an oasis, pyramids, stonehenge, a dead giant oak and a ruined church at the back of the catacombs. They looked right until you walked near them. Tile collision made you bump into empty air around a roof, and the player was drawn either fully in front of a building or fully behind it.

How I fixed it

Each building now reports two layers for every pixel: whether it's solid, and which ground row it stands on. The feet only collide with real footprints, so you can walk right up to a wall and behind a spire. Everything is drawn under the player first, then any pixel whose ground line is nearer than the player's feet is laid back over them. The same rule lets the oak's branches cover trees behind it. A doorway carves a short passage into the building, so you can only go in through the front, never from behind.

Above: a step pyramid. On sand the same entrance becomes an Egyptian one.

Oct 2 to Oct 4: Every enemy gets a face

Battles used to show a green box. Now each enemy has a sprite sheet: eight directions, each with its own idle animation, based on the creature's description in A Book of Creatures. It turns to face you and its hitbox scales with the sprite.

How they're made

Small enemies are drawn as text grids; bigger ones, with many parts, come from small Python generators sharing a library of shapes. Aseprite paints the frames, and the build checks the exported sheet pixel by pixel against what was drawn. The biggest creatures get five or eight idle frames instead of three, so the battle code now reads a frame count per sheet.

Sep 30: Dashes crawling over the ground

Fullscreen, thin dark dashes appeared across the grass and moved as I walked. Windowed they never showed up. Later, at zooms like 0.75x, the player wobbled by a pixel and the tile seams shimmered while scrolling.

How I found it

Fullscreen the frame was scaled by 2.25, so tile edges landed on quarter pixels and sometimes sampled the row of the neighbouring sprite in the tile sheet. I matched the bad rows pixel for pixel to the tree art above the grass. The fix was to draw each frame at 1:1 and scale it once. The wobble came from float rounding at fractional zooms, so the camera now keeps its position in whole screen pixels.

Aug 10 to Sep 29: Cliffs, four times

The cliffs started as flat brown tiles, then a nine-slice, then a set of drawing rules copied from how MOTHER 1 draws its cliffs. Each version broke somewhere new: corners halfway down a face, ground sealed inside walls, pillars nobody could reach.

What finally worked

Stop drawing cliffs by rule. The world now stamps whole landforms: big ones spliced from the full MOTHER 1 overworld at seams where every 2×2 block of sprites already exists in that map, small ones grown from my own island drawings with wave function collapse.

Sep 27: A world that wraps

I joined the map's edges so you can walk off one side and come back on the other. The edges met, but the land didn't: every noise pattern, smoothing filter and river still treated the edge as an edge, so the ground stopped dead at the seam.

The hard part

Every stage of generation had to treat the joined axis as a circle, from the coastline smoothing to the rivers stepping across. Then the biome mix had drifted, so I retuned it until eight test seeds were within a point of the old worlds (sand 4.3% against 4.6%). A census tool checks the seam's two edges against every other pair of neighbouring lines.

Above: the whole world, one pixel to the tile. Fifteen settlements and the roads joining them.

Aug 14: Sixteen seconds to build a world

The player waits while the world generates, and it took 15.6 seconds.

How I fixed it

I timed every stage first. One pass asked "is there snow within fifty tiles?" for every desert tile, a 101×101 scan each time; it now runs once for the whole map. A smoothing filter recounted its 49-tile window for every tile, 17 times over 9 million tiles; it now slides the window instead. 15.6 seconds became 2.9, and a hash of all nine million tiles proved each change built the exact same world.

Aug 9: Towns in the middle of nowhere

Moats, winding paths and buildings kept showing up out in the wilderness, with working doors into real interiors that nobody had placed.

How I found it

Reading the code didn't find it; my first theory was wrong. So I made the generator snapshot the map after each of its two dozen stages and diffed them. The town stage was reading two empty town layouts that were declared one row tall, so the loop read about 97 KB past a 624-byte array, straight into the real starting town's layout, shifted by a row. That's why the junk looked hand-made.

Aug 7: Enemies stacking on each other

Enemies chasing you through a cave merged into one sprite. My separation pass pushed overlapping pairs apart twice per frame, and it still wasn't enough.

How I measured it

A stress test over 900 frames: in a one-tile corridor the closest two enemies sat 0.42 pixels apart, against a 14-pixel sprite. Each sweep only moves a push one link along a queue, so I sweep until nothing overlaps (at most 32 times). Closest approach went to about 15 pixels in every test, with zero overlapping frames.
